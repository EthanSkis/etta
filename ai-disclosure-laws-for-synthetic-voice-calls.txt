Legal disclosure requirements
for an AI wellbeing-call product
(US & UK)
Bottom line: a compliant version of this product is realistic to
build in both the US and the UK — but only if it discloses that
the caller is AI and obtains consent from the elderly recipient
(not just the family who pays). No jurisdiction outright bans a
benign, human-indistinguishable AI voice, but the entire legal
architecture in the US and the transparency regimes in the UK/
EU are built around the principle that the person on the line
must be able to know they are talking to a machine. The design
goal of being "indistinguishable from a human" is precisely what
converts a low-risk product into a legally exposed one. The
leading competitors that have survived press scrutiny (inTouch,
ElderVoice) already disclose AI identity and gate calls on the
senior's own consent — which is both the ethical and the legal
safe harbor.
The single biggest legal constraint is not a disclosure statute but the
TCPA consent rule: because a daily AI call uses an "artificial or
prerecorded voice," it requires the prior express consent of the person
actually being called — and courts have held that a family member
cannot supply that consent unless they are the phone's subscriber/
customary user or an authorized agent of the recipient. That directly
complicates the "family member sets up the service" model.

US federal law: consent is mandatory now;
an explicit "this is AI" disclosure is
proposed but not yet law
AI-generated voices are already regulated under the TCPA. On
February 8, 2024, the FCC released a Declaratory Ruling (CG Docket
23-362, FCC 24-17) confirming that the TCPA's restrictions on "artificial
or prerecorded voice" encompass current AI technologies that
generate human voices, including voice cloning, and that such calls
"require the prior express consent of the called party to initiate such
calls absent an emergency purpose or exemption." fcc The ruling was
effective on release and explicitly refused to carve out technologies that
"purport to provide the equivalent of a live agent." fcc A conversational
AI check-in call is squarely within this definition.
What the TCPA requires today for such calls: (1) prior express
consent of the called party; (2) at the beginning of the message, a
clear statement of the identity of the entity responsible for the call; and
(3) during or after the message, the entity's telephone number or
address. cornell cornell So a caller-identity disclosure ("This is [service
name] calling on behalf of your family") is already legally required for
artificial-voice calls — but a statement that the voice is AI is not yet.
An explicit AI disclosure is only proposed, not in force. In an
NPRM published September 10, 2024 (FCC 24-84), the FCC proposed to
require callers to "clearly disclose... at the beginning of each call... that
the call is using AI-generated technology," plus AI-specific consent
disclosures. federalregister As of July 2026 this remains a proposed
rule — the current CFR text (47 CFR § 64.1200) still requires only caller
identity and number, not an AI disclosure, and no final Report and Order
adopting the AI-disclosure requirement was located. cornell
federalregister Ethan should design for this rule as if it will pass,
since the direction of federal policy is unambiguous and the product
would be an obvious target.

The telemarketing distinction helps but does not exempt the
product. The strictest rules (prior express written consent) attach to
calls that "include or introduce an advertisement or constitute
telemarketing." cornell A wellbeing check-in is not telemarketing, which
lowers the bar. However, the residential-landline exemption for non-
commercial artificial-voice calls only covers callers making no more
than three calls in any 30-day period — a daily call blows past that
limit, re-triggering the consent requirement. cornell So consent is
unavoidable regardless of the non-sales framing.
The consent problem the payer model creates. The TCPA requires
consent of the "called party." The FCC and courts define this as the
phone's current subscriber or its customary user, not merely the
intended recipient or a third party who set things up. epic The Eleventh
Circuit (Osorio) held that only the subscriber can consent, "either directly
or through an authorized agent," and that a housemate "had no
authority to consent in her own right." findlaw The Ninth Circuit (N.L. v.
Credit One) held that "consent from the intended recipient... is not
sufficient" — the actual called party must consent. uscourts
Implication: a family member can only validly consent if the elderly
person's phone is on the family member's account (making them the
subscriber) or the family member is acting as the recipient's authorized
agent. The safest design gets the elderly recipient's own consent —
which, notably, is exactly what inTouch does in practice (see below).
US state law: California is the binding
constraint; Texas and Florida are not
State coverage is uneven, and — importantly — several of the newer
AI-disclosure statutes are tied to "trade/commerce," "consumer
transactions," or "regulated occupations," meaning their
applicability to a paid-but-non-sales wellbeing call is arguable. The
clearest exposure is California.

Texas and Florida impose no applicable AI-voice disclosure
requirement. Texas SB 140 (effective September 1, 2025) — which the
command flagged — turns out on inspection of the enrolled text to
contain no AI/artificial-voice disclosure requirement at all; it
amends telephone-solicitation definitions and seller-registration rules,
and "telephone solicitation" is defined as a call to induce a person to
"purchase, rent, claim, or receive an item." texas A non-sales wellbeing
call falls outside Chapter 302 entirely. texas Texas's synthesized-voice
consent rules are likewise tied to solicitation contexts. Florida's
telemarketing statute prohibits disguising a caller's voice "to defraud,
confuse, or injure," and regulates automated sales calls, but has no
general AI-disclosure mandate for a benign check-in. flsenate So the
two named states are not the constraint — California is.
The California AB 2602 / SB 1381 lead was a false trail. AB 2602
governs contract enforceability for "digital replicas" of performers' voices
in entertainment/creative work — not triggered by an AI wellbeing call.
leginfo SB 1381 expands child-sexual-abuse-material criminal law to AI-
generated content — wholly irrelevant here. leginfo The canvas note
should be corrected: the relevant California statute is AB 2905, not
those two.
FTC exposure is about deception, not disclosure per se. The FTC's
Impersonation Rule (16 CFR Part 461, effective April 1, 2024) targets
falsely posing as a government entity or business ecfr; the
Telemarketing Sales Rule governs sales calls. ecfr The FTC has flagged AI
voice cloning as a deception/enforcement priority under its Section 5
authority. ftc None of this bans a disclosed AI voice, but it confirms that

an undisclosed, human-mimicking AI voice is the fact pattern regulators
are hunting.
UK law: no AI-voice-specific rule, but
transparency principles apply and PECR
probably doesn't bite
The UK has no Ofcom rule specifically requiring disclosure that a
call is AI-generated; Ofcom's telephone guidance addresses nuisance/
silent calls and marketing identity/opt-out, not AI voices. ofcom Notably,
the ICO in September 2025 fined two energy firms for unlawful
marketing calls that used "avatar software" to make recipients think
they were speaking to named humans — a signal that human-mimicking
AI voices draw regulatory scrutiny, though that case was a marketing
PECR breach. ico
PECR's automated-call rules likely do not apply, because they are
limited to direct marketing. The ICO states that "routine customer
service messages do not count as direct marketing" and that the
definition doesn't cover live calls for "administrative or customer service
purposes." ico PECR Regulation 19's consent requirement applies to
"recorded matter for direct marketing purposes" via automated calling
systems. legislation A genuine, non-promotional wellbeing call therefore
falls outside the strict automated-marketing consent regime — a
meaningful advantage over the US position. This holds only if the calls
never carry promotional content or harvest data for future marketing.
UK GDPR / ICO transparency still applies because the product
processes personal (and health-adjacent) data. The ICO requires
processing to be "lawful, fair and transparent," and states processing is
"unlikely to be considered transparent" if the organisation is not open
about how AI is used. ico ico Recording conversations requires telling
people it's happening. ico This is a transparency/consent obligation, not
an "announce AI" mandate, but disclosing AI identity is the natural way
to satisfy fairness for a cognitively vulnerable data subject.

Consumer protection and the Online Safety Act. Under the Digital
Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, a "misleading omission"
requires an omission of material information within a "commercial
practice" affecting a transactional decision legislation — no authority
treats an undisclosed AI voice in a non-marketing call as per se unfair,
though aggressive non-disclosure to a vulnerable person could
theoretically be argued. The Online Safety Act 2023 does not apply:
it regulates user-to-user and search internet services and expressly
exempts services whose only user content is "one-to-one live aural
communications." legislation legislation
The "indistinguishable AI" question: no
jurisdiction bans it, but disclosure regimes
make hiding it the offence
Across the US, UK, and EU, there is no law making it illegal to use a
human-indistinguishable AI voice for a benign purpose per se.
The illegality in every regime attaches to non-disclosure or intent to
deceive, not to the quality of the voice:
• US state laws (Maine, Colorado, Utah, CA SB 1001) criminalize/
penalize the failure to disclose or intent to mislead — not
indistinguishability itself. maine justia leginfo
• The UK Fraud Act 2006 requires dishonesty plus intent to make a gain
or cause loss — a benign check-in lacks those elements. legislation
• The EU AI Act Article 50 (transparency, applying from August 2,
2026) requires that AI systems interacting directly with people be
designed so persons are "informed that they are interacting with an
AI system unless this is obvious." europa europa An indistinguishable
voice is exactly the case where disclosure becomes mandatory rather
than optional (because it is no longer "obvious").
The pattern is consistent: the more indistinguishable the voice, the
stronger the legal duty to disclose it. Designing for indistinguishability is
legally fine; designing to conceal AI identity is what creates liability. For a

cognitively impaired recipient (Ethan's originating use case), non-
disclosure also raises the sharpest ethical and consent risk — the 404
Media critique's core concern.
How competitors handle it: the market
leaders already disclose
Vendor practice validates the compliant path and shows disclosure is not
a market disadvantage:
• inTouch discloses explicitly. Its founder states "in the first call... we
clearly say this is an AI" and that they disclose "constantly" that
"Mary is an AI," and its FAQ states "No calls to seniors ever start
without receiving the senior's explicit consent first." mashable
intouch This consent-gating maps directly onto the TCPA "called-party
consent" requirement.
• ElderVoice discloses in its FAQ ("Is a real person calling? No,
ElderVoice is an advanced Artificial Intelligence") and introduces itself
as a companion service "set up by you (the family member)" on the
first call. eldervoice
• Verocall states "We are not trying to fool anyone into thinking our
agents are human" and even tells families to reconsider if the loved
one cannot tell it's not human. verocall verocall
• Joy Calls identifies itself as AI on its site but appears to rely on family
pre-briefing ("talk to your loved one about Joy in advance") rather
than a forced start-of-call label, while designing the voice to "sound
and feel human." joycalls
• CareCall and Senior Protection AI do not surface any explicit
senior-facing AI disclosure or start-of-call script in their public
materials — the weakest-disclosure end of the market. intouch
The 404 Media test underscores why disclosure matters: the reporter's
mother perceived the call as "an automated company cold call," and a
Harvard ethicist warned AI caregiving must be approached with
"humility, honesty." theverge ispr Caregiver-community sentiment is

actively hostile to undisclosed AI ("I don't want my elderly parents
talking to AI"). reddit Disclosure is thus both a legal hedge and a trust/
reputation necessity.
What this means for the build
A compliant product is realistic in both the US and UK, and the
compliant design is already the market-leading design.
Concretely, the safe configuration is:
1. Disclose AI identity at the start of every call ("Hi, this is [name],
an AI assistant calling to check in on behalf of your family"). This is
not yet federally mandated but is (a) proposed by the FCC
federalregister, (b) required by Maine for voice AI in commerce
maine, Colorado from mid-2026 justia, and California's AB 2905
announcement structure leginfo, (c) required under the EU AI Act
from August 2026 europa, and (d) demanded by Utah on request
utah. One disclosure line satisfies all of them.
2. Also state caller identity and a callback number — already
required federally for artificial-voice calls today. cornell
3. Obtain the elderly recipient's own consent, not just the family's,
because the "called party" is the recipient and third-party consent is
legally shaky unless the family member is the phone's subscriber or
authorized agent. findlaw uscourts This is the deepest tension with
the "family buys it" model and needs a real consent-capture step
involving the senior.
4. Keep call content strictly non-promotional to stay outside US
telemarketing rules and UK PECR marketing consent. ico
5. Handle recording transparency and health-data processing
under UK GDPR/US state privacy rules (disclose recording; lawful
basis for health-adjacent data). ico
The "indistinguishable from a human" design ambition is the one
element to walk back: build a natural, non-robotic voice, but front it with

an honest disclosure and recipient consent. That is legal, ethical, and —
per inTouch and ElderVoice — commercially normal.
Where further research would most change the conclusion: First,
the recipient-consent mechanics deserve a dedicated legal review
with a TCPA attorney — specifically whether a workable consent flow can
be built when the buyer and the called party differ, and whether the
senior's verbal consent captured on the first AI call itself suffices (a
chicken-and-egg problem the case law doesn't cleanly resolve). Second,
the FCC AI-disclosure NPRM status should be re-checked close to
launch: if it is finalized, start-of-call AI disclosure shifts from "best
practice" to a hard federal requirement, and the exact mandated
wording/timing would need to be matched precisely.
